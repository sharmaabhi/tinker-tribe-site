Motor Types: Brushed vs Coreless vs Brushless

Which motor goes in which project, and why each one needs a different kind of driver.

A quick reference for choosing the right motor type — all three show up across robot and plane builds, so here's what actually distinguishes them.

The Core Distinction

All three are DC motors at heart, but they differ in how the rotor is built and how current gets switched to make it spin.

Regular Brushed Coreless Brushed Brushless
Rotor construction Copper windings wound around an iron core Copper windings with no iron core — just the windings themselves, hollow/cup-shaped No brushes at all — magnets on the rotor, windings fixed on the outside (stator)
How current reaches the rotor Physical carbon brushes contacting a spinning commutator Same — physical brushes and commutator No physical contact — an ESC electronically switches current through the stationary windings
Wires from motor 2 (simple +/−, polarity determines direction) 2 (same) 3 (needs a matched ESC to drive)

1. Regular Brushed Motor (with iron core)

What it is: the classic, simplest DC motor — windings wrapped around a solid iron core, with brushes physically touching a rotating commutator to switch current direction as it spins.

Pros: cheap, simple, works with any basic driver (a plain H-bridge like your TB6612FNG/L298N), widely available.

Cons: the iron core adds real weight and rotational inertia (slower to speed up/ slow down), brushes physically wear out over time (limited lifespan), less efficient than the alternatives — some energy is lost to the core's magnetic resistance.

In your builds: your BO motors (ground robot) are this type — simple, cheap, good for learning/validating wiring and control logic before moving to something more capable.

2. Coreless Brushed Motor

What it is: same brush/commutator switching mechanism as a regular brushed motor, but the rotor's windings have no iron core — just a lightweight, hollow winding structure. This dramatically reduces rotor weight and rotational inertia.

Pros: much lighter, spins up and slows down almost instantly (very low inertia), more efficient than an iron-core brushed motor, genuinely tiny sizes are practical.

Cons: generally lower torque/power ceiling than an iron-core motor of similar size, brushes still wear out over time (shorter-lived than brushless), still needs a simple brushed-motor-style driver (though at much lower current than a full-size brushed motor).

In your builds: your 8520 motor (plane build) is this type — exactly why it's the standard choice for tiny, weight-critical "slow flyer" aircraft. The near-zero rotational inertia also makes throttle response feel snappier despite the motor's small size.

3. Brushless Motor

What it is: flips the whole arrangement — permanent magnets are mounted on the rotor (the spinning part), and the windings are fixed on the outer stator (the stationary part). Since the windings don't move, there's no need for brushes at all — instead, an ESC electronically switches current through the three stator windings in a precise sequence to keep the rotor spinning (this sequencing is called "commutation").

Pros: no brushes to wear out (much longer lifespan), generally more efficient, much better power-to-weight ratio, handles higher speeds and higher power reliably.

Cons: needs a genuinely more complex driver (a brushless ESC, not a simple H-bridge), more expensive per motor, overkill for very small/low-power applications.

In your builds: your A2212/1400KV and MT2204/2300KV motors are this type — the step up in capability (and complexity) once you move beyond simple hobby-grade builds into more serious RC planes or drones.

Quick Decision Guide

If you need... Choose
Cheapest option, simple wiring, don't mind some weight/inefficiency Regular brushed
Extremely light, tiny, fast throttle response, low power draw Coreless brushed
Best efficiency, longest lifespan, higher power, willing to add ESC complexity Brushless
A first "just get it moving" test before committing to better hardware Regular brushed (cheapest to experiment with)
A genuinely capable final RC plane or drone build Brushless

How the Driver Requirement Differs — the Practical Takeaway

  • Regular brushed & Coreless brushed: both use the same simple 2-wire driving approach — a basic H-bridge (TB6612FNG, L298N) or an even simpler single-direction brushed ESC (like your iFuturetech module) works for either. Swapping between a regular brushed motor and a coreless one on the same driver is a drop-in change.
  • Brushless: requires a purpose-built brushless ESC that understands 3-phase commutation — you cannot drive a brushless motor with a brushed-motor driver, and vice versa. This is exactly why brushless motors like the MT2204/A2212 need a completely different ESC (like the EMAX Lightning) than your 8520 coreless motor's simple brushed ESC.
