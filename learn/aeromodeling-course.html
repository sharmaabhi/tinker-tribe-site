1. Introduction to Aeromodeling

Aeromodeling is the hobby of designing, building, and flying model aircraft — ranging from a folded sheet of paper to GPS-guided autonomous drones. It sits at the intersection of physics, engineering, and craft, and it rewards understanding why something flies, not just following a build guide blindly.

This course is organized to build knowledge progressively: physics first, then shapes and parts, then hands-on paper models, then real powered aircraft, then advanced and autonomous systems. Each section stands alone enough to reference later, but reading in order builds a coherent mental model.

2. The Physics of Flight (Basics)

The Four Forces

Every aircraft, from a paper dart to a jumbo jet, is governed by the same four forces:

Force Direction Source
Lift Upward (perpendicular to airflow) Wing shape + speed of air over it
Weight (Gravity) Downward Mass of the aircraft
Thrust Forward Motor/propeller, or hand-launch momentum
Drag Backward Air resistance against the aircraft's shape

For steady, level flight: Lift = Weight, and Thrust = Drag. Any imbalance causes climbing, diving, accelerating, or decelerating.

How Lift Actually Works

The classic explanation is Bernoulli's principle: air moving faster over the curved top of a wing has lower pressure than the slower-moving air underneath, creating a net upward force. A complementary and equally valid explanation is Newton's third law: the wing deflects air downward, and the reaction pushes the wing upward. Both are true simultaneously — they're different lenses on the same phenomenon, not competing theories.

Angle of Attack

The angle between the wing's chord line and the oncoming airflow. Increasing this angle increases lift — up to a point. Beyond a critical angle, airflow separates from the wing's upper surface and lift collapses. This is a stall — the single most important failure mode to understand and avoid, especially at low speed (takeoff, landing, sharp turns).

Key Terms

  • Chord: the front-to-back width of a wing, measured at any given point along the span
  • Span: wingtip to wingtip distance
  • Airfoil: the cross-sectional shape of a wing, viewed from the side
  • Camber: the curvature of an airfoil — more camber generally means more lift at low speed, at the cost of more drag

3. Wing Shapes, Tail Types & Aircraft Configurations

Wing Planform Shapes

Shape Characteristics Common Use
Rectangular Simple to build, forgiving stall behavior, moderate efficiency Trainers, beginner builds
Tapered Narrower at the tip than the root — better efficiency, slightly trickier stall behavior Sport planes, many scale models
Elliptical Most aerodynamically efficient shape theoretically, but hardest to build Classic WWII fighters (Spitfire), rarely used in hobby builds due to complexity
Delta Wide triangular shape, very stable at a range of speeds, low aspect ratio Fast jets, some flying wings
Swept Wingtips angled backward High-speed aircraft, reduces drag at speed

Aspect Ratio — The Single Most Important Wing Number

Aspect Ratio = Span² ÷ Wing Area (or approximately Span ÷ Average Chord for a simple rectangular wing)

  • High aspect ratio (long, narrow wings): efficient, glides well, less drag — gliders and slow-flyers use this
  • Low aspect ratio (short, wide wings): more structurally rigid, more maneuverable, more drag — aerobatic and combat aircraft use this

Tail Configurations

Type Layout Notes
Conventional Horizontal stabilizer + vertical fin, standard "T" from behind Most common, well-understood handling
T-Tail Horizontal stabilizer mounted on top of the vertical fin Keeps tail clear of wing wake, common on gliders
V-Tail Two angled surfaces replace separate horizontal/vertical tails, combining elevator+rudder function Lighter, less drag, slightly more complex control mixing
Flying Wing No separate tail at all — the wing itself provides all control Very efficient, but pitch stability is harder to achieve; needs careful design (sweep, reflex airfoil)

Fuselage Types

  • Pod-and-boom: a compact body (pod) with a thin boom extending back to the tail — common on gliders and trainers, lightweight
  • Full fuselage: a complete body shape, often for scale-realistic models
  • Flat-plate (foam board): the simplest DIY construction — flat sheet fuselage, functional rather than realistic (this is what your own foam builds use)

4. Key Flight Characteristics

Center of Gravity (CG)

The point at which the aircraft's weight is balanced. For most conventional designs, CG sits at roughly 25-33% back from the wing's leading edge. Too far forward = nose heavy, hard to get the nose up; too far back = tail heavy, unstable, prone to stalling unpredictably.

Dihedral

The upward angle of the wings when viewed from the front (a shallow "V" shape). Dihedral adds roll stability — if the plane banks unintentionally, dihedral naturally helps level it back out. This is especially valuable on simple planes without ailerons (rudder-only or elevator+rudder-only designs), since it substitutes for active roll control.

Wing Loading

Wing Loading = Weight ÷ Wing Area

Lower wing loading = slower flying speed, gentler stall, more floaty character (good for beginners and indoor flying). Higher wing loading = faster, more responsive, but less forgiving. This is why a heavier plane with the same wing needs to fly faster to generate enough lift — directly relevant to why battery/component weight matters so much on small builds.

Stability vs. Maneuverability — A Fundamental Tradeoff

Every design decision trades off between these two. A stable plane (docile, self- correcting, good for beginners) is inherently less maneuverable. A highly maneuverable plane (aerobatic, agile) is inherently less forgiving of pilot error. There's no "best" setting — only what's appropriate for the plane's intended purpose and pilot skill level.

5. Paper Airplane Designs (Hands-On Starting Point)

Paper planes are the fastest, cheapest way to feel these principles before committing to a powered build. Three designs, each teaching something different:

Design 1: The Dart

A narrow, pointed design (fold a sheet in half lengthwise, fold the top corners into the center crease repeatedly to form a sharp point, then fold the wings down along the body). Teaches: low aspect ratio + high wing loading = fast, far-flying, but not very stable in gusty air.

Design 2: The Glider (Wide-Wing)

Wider wings, blunter nose, often with small upturned wingtips (a paper equivalent of dihedral). Teaches: higher aspect ratio + dihedral = slower, more stable, better glide ratio (distance traveled per unit of height lost).

Design 3: The Delta Wing

A simple triangular shape, folded from a square or rectangular sheet into a wide triangle with a center spine. Teaches: how a delta shape achieves stability across a wide range of speeds without a separate tail — the same principle used in real delta-wing aircraft.

Suggested exercise: build all three from the same size paper, throw each the same way, and observe the difference in distance, stability, and glide angle. This is a genuine, hands-on demonstration of aspect ratio and wing loading — cheaper and faster than any powered experiment.

6. Understanding the Parts of an RC Plane

Part Function
Wing Generates lift
Fuselage Structural body, houses electronics/battery
Horizontal Stabilizer / Elevator Controls pitch (nose up/down)
Vertical Fin / Rudder Controls yaw (nose left/right)
Ailerons (on the wing, if present) Controls roll (banking left/right)
Motor Provides thrust
ESC (Electronic Speed Controller) Regulates power delivered to the motor based on throttle input
Propeller Converts motor rotation into thrust
Servo Small motor that moves control surfaces (elevator, rudder, ailerons) based on receiver signals
Receiver Receives radio signals from the transmitter, outputs corresponding servo/ESC commands
Transmitter The pilot's handheld radio control unit
Battery Power source — almost always LiPo for RC aircraft due to high power-to-weight ratio

Control Surface Effects, Summarized

  • Elevator up → nose pitches up → climb (or stall if too abrupt/slow)
  • Elevator down → nose pitches down → dive
  • Rudder left/right → yaws the nose left/right (also assists turning on rudder-only planes)
  • Aileron left/right → banks the plane, the primary way real turns are coordinated on aileron-equipped planes

7. Motors & Propulsion Systems

Motor Types

Type Characteristics Best For
Coreless brushed Very light, low power, low cost Ultra-micro indoor planes
Standard brushed (with iron core) Cheap, simple, less efficient, motor wears out faster Toy-grade and entry builds
Brushless No brushes to wear out, higher efficiency, more power-dense Almost all serious RC planes and drones today

Understanding KV Rating (Brushless Motors)

KV = RPM per volt applied, with no load. A 2300KV motor on a 2S (7.4V) battery spins at roughly 2300 × 7.4 ≈ 17,000 RPM unloaded.

  • High KV (e.g., 2500KV+): spins fast, pairs with small/lightweight propellers — common on small, fast planes and racing drones
  • Low KV (e.g., under 1000KV): spins slower but with more torque, pairs with large propellers — common on larger, slower-flying planes

Propeller Sizing Basics

Propellers are specified as diameter × pitch (e.g., "8×6" = 8 inches diameter, 6 inches of theoretical forward travel per rotation).

  • Larger diameter: more thrust at low speed, but more drag and higher current draw
  • Higher pitch: more speed potential, but more strain on the motor (like a higher gear on a bicycle)
  • Motor and propeller must be matched — an underpowered motor with too large/high-pitch a prop will overheat or stall; an oversized motor with too small a prop wastes potential power

Electric Ducted Fans (EDF) and Other Propulsion

For scale jet-look models, an EDF (a shrouded fan, rather than an open propeller) simulates a jet engine's look and sound, at the cost of efficiency compared to an open prop. Glow (nitro-fuel) and gas engines exist for larger, traditional hobby aircraft but are far less common for beginners today given the convenience of electric power.

8. Materials & Construction Methods

Material Properties Use Case
EPP Foam Very durable, crash-resistant, slightly heavier Trainers, durable park flyers
Depron / Foam board Light, cheap, easy to cut with a hobby knife DIY scratch-builds, beginner-friendly
Balsa wood Very light, traditional material, needs more skill/time to build and finish Classic kit builds, scale models
Carbon fiber (rods/plates) Extremely stiff and light, but expensive Reinforcement in high-performance builds
3D printed (PLA/PETG) Precise, repeatable, good for complex small parts (mounts, brackets) Motor mounts, custom fittings, not usually entire airframes (too heavy in bulk)

General principle: every gram matters disproportionately on small aircraft. A material choice that's "slightly heavier but stronger" is often the wrong tradeoff on a small indoor plane, but the right one on a larger, more robust outdoor design.

9. Advanced Fixed-Wing Aircraft

Aerobatic Planes

Designed for 3D flight (hovering, tumbling, extreme maneuvers) — typically low wing loading combined with high power-to-weight ratio, symmetrical airfoils (equal lift capability upside-down as right-side-up), and large control surface deflections.

FPV (First-Person-View) Planes

Equipped with an onboard camera transmitting live video to the pilot's goggles or screen, allowing "flying from the cockpit" perspective without needing to see the actual aircraft. Requires a video transmitter, camera, and a separate receiver/goggle system alongside the normal RC control link.

Scale Models

Built to accurately represent a real aircraft's proportions, often with retractable landing gear, detailed panel lines, and sometimes multiple engines — prioritizes realism over pure flight performance.

Gliders / Soaring Planes

Extremely high aspect ratio wings, minimal drag, often unpowered (winch-launched or hand-tossed) or with a small motor just to gain initial altitude before switching off and soaring on thermals (rising warm air currents) — a specialized, skill-intensive discipline in its own right.

10. Multirotor Drones

A fundamentally different flight principle from fixed-wing aircraft: instead of a wing generating lift from forward airflow, multiple motors with propellers generate direct vertical thrust, and flight control comes entirely from varying each motor's speed.

Common Configurations

Type Motor Count Notes
Tricopter 3 Rare, needs a servo-tilted rear motor for yaw control
Quadcopter 4 Most common — simple, efficient, widely supported
Hexacopter 6 More redundancy (can survive one motor failure), more lift capacity
Octocopter 8 Maximum lift capacity and redundancy, used for heavy-lift/cinema drones

Key Components (Beyond What Fixed-Wing Planes Use)

  • Flight Controller (FC): a small onboard computer with a gyroscope and accelerometer, constantly adjusting each motor's speed many times per second to keep the drone stable — this is the component that makes multirotor flight possible at all, since a human cannot manually balance 4+ motors in real time
  • ESCs (one per motor): unlike a single-motor plane, drones need one ESC per motor, often combined onto a single "4-in-1 ESC" board for quadcopters
  • Frame: the structural X or H-shaped body holding all motors at fixed positions

Why Drones Are Easier to Fly (But Harder to Build Well)

The flight controller's automatic stabilization means a beginner can hover a drone almost immediately, unlike a fixed-wing plane which requires understanding stall speed, banking, and continuous small corrections. However, building/tuning a multirotor (motor matching, ESC calibration, flight controller PID tuning) is arguably more technically involved than a simple fixed-wing build.

11. Autonomous & "AI" Aircraft

What "Autonomous" Actually Means Here

Rather than a human continuously operating the controls, the aircraft uses sensors and onboard logic to fly some or all of a mission itself. This spans a wide range:

Level Capability
Stabilization only Gyro/accelerometer smooths pilot input, still fully manually flown
Altitude/Position Hold Aircraft holds a set altitude or GPS position without constant pilot correction
Waypoint Navigation Pilot sets GPS points on a map; aircraft flies the route autonomously
Full autonomy / obstacle avoidance Aircraft uses cameras/sensors (computer vision) to detect and react to its environment without pre-programmed waypoints

The Core Hardware Stack

  • Flight Controller running real autopilot firmware: open-source options like ArduPilot and PX4 (running on hardware like a Pixhawk, or the Matek F722 boards we discussed for a fixed-wing build) provide the actual autonomous flight logic — PID control loops, navigation, failsafe behavior
  • GPS module: provides absolute position, essential for waypoint navigation and return-to-home functionality
  • IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit): gyroscope + accelerometer (sometimes + magnetometer and barometer) for orientation and altitude sensing
  • Companion computer (optional, for genuine "AI" behavior): a small onboard computer (like a Raspberry Pi or an NVIDIA Jetson) running computer vision models — object detection, obstacle avoidance, visual navigation — that sends high-level commands to the flight controller, which still handles the actual low-level flight stabilization

The Two-Brain Architecture (Same Principle as a Ground Robot)

This mirrors exactly the same "ESP32 handles reflexes, Pi handles thinking" split discussed for ground robots:

Companion computer (Pi/Jetson): camera, object detection, high-level decisions
              │
              ▼ (sends simple commands: "move to X,Y" or "avoid obstacle")
Flight Controller (ArduPilot/PX4): actual motor/servo control, stabilization, safety

The flight controller should always retain final authority over safety-critical behavior (failsafe, battery voltage cutoffs, stabilization) — the same principle as keeping obstacle-avoidance reflexes on the ESP32 rather than solely dependent on a higher-level, potentially slower or disconnected companion computer.

DIY Entry Points

  • madflight (ESP32-based, open source): a genuinely accessible way to build a basic autonomous flight controller from cheap parts, supporting fixed-wing mode with IMU stabilization and optional GPS
  • ArduPilot / PX4 on dedicated hardware (Pixhawk-class boards, or Matek F-series): the more mature, capable, and widely-supported path once you outgrow a DIY board

12. Suggested Learning Path

  1. Fold and fly the three paper plane designs — build direct intuition for aspect ratio, wing loading, and stability with zero cost or risk
  2. Build a simple foam glider, unpowered — hand-launch only, focus purely on CG and trim, no motor/electronics complexity yet
  3. Add basic power (a cheap coreless or brushed motor setup) — introduce throttle control, understand how thrust interacts with the airframe
  4. Move to a proper brushless-powered plane with full 3-axis control (elevator, rudder, ailerons) — this is "graduating" to a genuinely capable RC aircraft
  5. Explore one advanced direction based on interest: aerobatics, FPV, scale modeling, or gliding/soaring
  6. If drones interest you: start with a small, cheap quadcopter kit to learn multirotor-specific concepts (PID tuning, flight controller setup) before attempting a scratch-build
  7. If autonomy interests you: start with adding GPS/altitude-hold to an already reliably-flying manual aircraft — never attempt autonomous features on an unproven airframe or control setup

This mirrors the same principle used throughout this course: each stage should be proven and reliable before adding the next layer of complexity — the single most important practical lesson in aeromodeling, whether building a paper dart or a GPS-guided autonomous plane.
